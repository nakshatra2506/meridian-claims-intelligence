# Medically Unnecessary and Excessive Services

Services that were genuinely delivered, but should not have been — or not in
that quantity. This is where **waste, abuse and fraud overlap most**, and where
an automated system is least able to draw conclusions.

---

## Medically unnecessary services

**What it means.** Services not reasonable and necessary for the diagnosis or
treatment of the patient's condition, or not meeting the payer's coverage
criteria. Examples: diagnostic testing without clinical indication; treatment
frequency beyond what the condition supports; higher-intensity settings than
required; continued therapy after documented plateau.

**Why it may be suspicious.** The same pattern may reflect clinical caution,
defensive medicine, poor practice, or a revenue-driven scheme. Separating them
requires clinical judgement — this is the category where clinician review is
most essential and statistical evidence is weakest.

**How it appears in claims data.**

- Service frequency per beneficiary far above peer norms for the same diagnosis.
- Diagnostic tests without a supporting diagnosis on the claim.
- Long treatment episodes without documented change in patient condition.
- Uniform treatment plans applied across clinically dissimilar patients.
- Testing patterns that never influence subsequent treatment decisions.
- Higher-intensity settings where lower-intensity care is the norm.

**Possible legitimate explanations.**

- Complex or comorbid patients genuinely requiring intensive management.
- Clinical guidelines that mandate frequent monitoring.
- Specialty or referral-centre practice concentrating difficult cases.
- Necessity documented in the record but not visible on the claim.
- Evolving standards of care and legitimate clinical disagreement.
- Defensive medicine driven by liability concerns rather than revenue.

**What an investigator should examine.**

1. Clinical documentation supporting the indication for each service.
2. Whether test results affected subsequent care decisions.
3. Applicable coverage policies and clinical guidelines.
4. Whether care intensity varies with patient condition or is uniform.
5. **Qualified clinical review** — necessity is a clinical determination and
   must not be decided on statistical grounds alone.

---

## Excessive services (overutilization)

**What it means.** Services delivered or billed in quantities beyond what the
condition warrants — more units, more visits, more frequently, or for longer
than clinical need supports. The individual service may be appropriate; the
quantity is not.

**Why it may be suspicious.** Volume is the simplest way to increase revenue
without changing service mix, and it produces a distinctive fingerprint:
quantities cluster at payment or policy limits rather than distributing
naturally around clinical need.

**How it appears in claims data.**

- Units per service consistently at or near the allowable maximum.
- Visit counts per beneficiary well above the peer distribution.
- Episode lengths substantially longer than typical for the diagnosis.
- Little variance in service quantity across clinically different patients.
- Utilization that stops abruptly at a benefit limit rather than at recovery.

**Possible legitimate explanations.**

- Severely ill populations legitimately requiring intensive services.
- Care models built around frequent contact — chronic disease management,
  intensive rehabilitation, behavioural health programs.
- Guideline-driven protocols specifying high service frequency.
- Small denominators making per-beneficiary averages unstable.
- Facility or program types where high intensity is the standard.

**What an investigator should examine.**

1. The *distribution* of units and visits, not just averages — clustering at
   limits is more informative than the mean.
2. Whether service quantity correlates with documented patient severity.
3. Whether outcomes or documented progress justify continued intensity.
4. Whether the pattern concentrates in a few beneficiaries or spans the panel.
5. Peer benchmarks built on correctly matched practice type.
